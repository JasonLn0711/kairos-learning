# CEH M09-03 | Vishing, Smishing, and Impersonation

Module 09: Social Engineering | Beginner lesson | About 12–15 minutes

The central idea of this lesson: attackers do not always need to break into your computer. If they make you believe they are someone trustworthy, they may persuade you to carry out an unsafe action yourself.

Also, receiving two messages that appear to confirm each other does not establish two independent, trustworthy sources.

Using the fictional Northstar Learning Center, we will work through these questions:

1. How do attackers combine text messages, phone calls, and false identities?
2. Why can a genuine login code become useful to an attacker?
3. Why is independent verification more reliable than trying to recognize a fake voice?
4. How can security staff connect concepts from other CEH units to analyze, prevent, and investigate the attack?

## 1. Start with the whole picture: four different dimensions of phishing

The previous unit, M09-02, introduced phishing, spear phishing, and whaling.

This lesson adds three terms:

- Smishing: phishing that uses SMS text messages to manipulate the recipient.
- Vishing: phishing that uses telephone or other voice communication to manipulate the recipient.
- Impersonation: causing a person to mistake an attacker for another person or organization.

These are not mutually exclusive attack categories. They answer different questions.

Figure 1 | The four-dimensional attack-analysis framework used in this lesson.

For example, an attacker might gather background information about Lin, design a text specifically for him, then call while pretending to be company support and ask for a verification code.

If there is actual evidence that the approach was tailored to him, we can describe it as:

Spear phishing (target) + smishing and vishing (channels) + impersonation (technique) + obtaining a verification code (objective).

A common exam mistake: an attacker claiming to be a chairperson does not automatically make the incident whaling. Whaling primarily concerns targeting senior or high-value people, not impersonating a senior person.

The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security also places smishing and vishing among related forms of social engineering and phishing. [Canadian Centre for Cyber Security: Social engineering](https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/social-engineering-itsap00166); [Vishing](https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/what-voice-phishing-vishing-itsap00102); [Smishing](https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/smishing-protect-yourself-sms-attacks-itsap00103)

## 2. Vishing and smishing: two channels may have one attacker behind them

Imagine that you are Lin at Northstar Learning Center.

At 10 a.m., you receive a text:

> [Northstar IT Help Desk] An unusual login has been detected on your course-materials account. Contact support promptly to avoid account suspension.

Two minutes later, your phone rings.

The caller says, “Hello, Lin. This is the IT help desk. There is an issue with the account for the course-materials project you are managing. We should have just sent you a text.”

Lin thinks, “They know my name, my work, and even that I just received a text.”

But this is what might actually be happening:

Figure 2 | An attacker can control both the text message and the phone call, creating an illusion of mutual confirmation.

This approach exploits an intuitive response: when consistent information appears in different places, we tend to treat it as more credible.

From a security perspective, however, the important question is whether the evidence is genuinely independent.

### Three technical ideas to distinguish

1. Caller ID spoofing

The name or number displayed for an incoming call can be falsified.

It is like writing “sent by a bank” on an envelope: that label does not establish that the bank really sent it.

The FTC warns against relying on caller ID alone to establish who is calling. [Federal Trade Commission: Scammers can fake caller ID info](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2016/05/scammers-can-fake-caller-id-info)

2. AI voice cloning

Attackers may also use AI to imitate the voice of a manager, family member, friend, or support worker. [FTC: AI voice-cloning scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/03/scammers-use-ai-enhance-their-family-emergency-schemes)

Keep these distinctions in mind:

- Sounding human does not prove that the voice is a live human voice.
- Sounding like a particular person does not prove that the speaker is that person.
- Distorted audio does not by itself prove that AI was used.

The real defensive strategy is therefore not to develop perfect hearing for every AI-generated voice. It is to establish identity-verification procedures that do not depend on the voice alone.

3. Reconnaissance and footprinting

This connects to concepts in CEH M02.

An attacker who knows Lin's name, department, and project may previously have collected that information from a company website, social posts, event lists, or similar sources.

If specific account, group, or shared-resource information was obtained by querying system services, the activity may also connect to enumeration in M04.

But keep a firm distinction:

Knowing someone's background does not prove that their account has been compromised. Knowing an internal project name also does not directly establish that information was leaked.

## 3. How can a genuine OTP help a fake support worker?

This is the technical mechanism most worth understanding in the lesson.

An OTP, or one-time password/verification code, is intended as a security measure.

This lesson uses OTP informally for the one-time login code in the example. Under NIST's formal classification, a code that a platform sends to a phone by SMS belongs to out-of-band authentication, which differs from an OTP generated by an authenticator. If people must manually enter either kind of output, they may still be deceived into relaying it. [NIST SP 800-63B-4: Authenticators and phishing resistance](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html#phishres)

Suppose you are logging into the course-materials system:

1. You enter your account name and password.
2. The system requires a second authentication factor.
3. The platform sends a one-time verification code to your phone.
4. You enter the code in the login process you initiated. Only after verification succeeds may the platform permit the login.

Under normal conditions, the OTP helps reduce the risk of account misuse.

An attacker can nevertheless use social engineering to try to persuade you to give them the code.

Figure 3 | Social engineering of a one-time verification code. This is a synthetic scenario; not every website uses the same authentication procedure.

### Why can the platform not identify the person from the OTP alone?

In plain language:

The platform checks whether the code is correct, valid, and consistent with its verification rules. That does not necessarily reveal whether the person entering it is the actual holder of the phone.

Suppose that:

- The attacker already has Lin's account name and password.
- The platform also requires an SMS verification code.
- Lin reads the code to the fake support worker.
- The attacker submits it to the genuine platform while it is still valid.

If the other authentication conditions are also satisfied, the attacker may successfully log in.

This corrects a common misconception: knowing an OTP does not always guarantee a successful login. Some systems impose device restrictions, additional verification, or risk checks. Different codes may also authorize only particular operations.

### Next, understand MFA and phishing-resistant authentication

MFA, or multi-factor authentication, requires different types of authentication factors.

For example:

| Authentication factor | Everyday example |
| --- | --- |
| Something you know | A password you know |
| Something you have | A phone or security key you possess |
| Something you are | A biometric characteristic, such as your fingerprint |

The key is not the number of steps, but whether different types of factors are used. Entering two passwords does not automatically make the process MFA.

NIST SP 800-63B-4 states that OTP and out-of-band authentication involving manual entry of an authenticator output are not phishing-resistant. Manual entry does not bind the output to the specific session being authenticated—a particular authentication interaction—so an attacker may relay it. [NIST SP 800-63B-4: Phishing resistance](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html#phishres)

Compare the two approaches:

Traditional OTP

The SMS code in this example expires and can be used only once, but it may still be relayed to an attacker in real time.

Phishing-resistant authentication

Correctly implemented FIDO2/WebAuthn authentication, for example, uses cryptographic mechanisms to bind authentication to the intended verifier.

Figure 4 | Phishing resistance is not simply an additional authentication step.

A passkey is a cryptographic sign-in credential based on FIDO standards, commonly used through WebAuthn. The protection of the complete service still depends on its implementation, account-recovery procedures, and usage context. Phishing-resistant authentication primarily prevents fake sites from exploiting login credentials; it does not automatically stop every fraudulent payment or improper authorization to release information. [FIDO Alliance: Passkeys](https://fidoalliance.org/passkeys/)

### Connect this to authentication and authorization

This is a fundamental distinction in security engineering:

- Authentication: Who are you? Can you demonstrate control of this account?
- Authorization: Once authenticated, what are you permitted to do?

It is like using a staff badge to enter an office: getting through the entrance does not mean you can open every manager's office or every records cabinet.

A successful login therefore does not authorize every operation. An attacker obtaining a verification code also does not establish that all information has been read.

## 4. Independent verification means leaving the suspicious source's control

Return to Lin's situation.

The fake support worker says, “If you do not believe me, call the support number in the text you just received.”

Lin does so. The person answering says, “Yes, there was an unusual-login support ticket.”

Has this verified the request?

No. The support number and the ticket information may all have come from the attacker.

Figure 5 | Independent verification depends on the source of trust, not on switching communication tools.

Suppose someone calls while impersonating your bank's support staff. A safer response is to end the suspicious call, then open the bank's official app that you already had installed, or use an official contact method you previously verified.

In Taiwan, people can also independently dial the 165 anti-fraud consultation hotline for help with suspected scams. It is an official consultation channel provided by the Criminal Investigation Bureau. [Criminal Investigation Bureau: 165 anti-fraud consultation hotline](https://www.cib.npa.gov.tw/ch/app/faq/view?id=18233&module=faq&serno=8306e5f3-6b7a-45e9-9d21-af24d4fac9af)

Remember one further distinction: even confirming that the caller really works for the company does not authorize every request they make.

For example, if a genuine IT employee asks you to send all employee records, you must still check the work purpose, permissions, and formal approval process.

Separate three questions: Is the identity genuine? Is the request genuine? Is the operation authorized?

## 5. Incident reasoning: sharing a code does not prove completed compromise

This connects directly to incident response in M01-07.

Suppose Lin tells security staff, “I received a call from fake support, and I think I read them my verification code.”

A security engineer cannot immediately write:

“The attacker has compromised the company, and all course materials have been disclosed.”

The current evidence supports a more limited conclusion.

Figure 6 | Incident developments and evidence levels. In practice, stages may overlap, be skipped, or remain unverified.

Now reason from the available evidence:

| Available information | Reasonable conclusion |
| --- | --- |
| A text-message screenshot and call record | There are signs of a message and call; the source's identity is not yet established |
| Lin reports having read out the OTP | Authentication information may have been exposed, requiring prompt action |
| The platform shows a successful login | A successful-login event exists; determine whether it was unauthorized |
| Unusual device access or download records | Access activity warrants investigation; identify the operator and purpose |
| Verified evidence of data transmission | This can further support a finding of data exfiltration and help establish its scope |

Risk reduction can begin before an investigation is complete, but final conclusions must not exceed the evidence.

If Lin really shared the code with a stranger, he should report promptly through the official help desk. Depending on the system, authorized staff can examine login records, terminate suspicious sessions, update necessary credentials, and preserve logs.

Absence of an observed login record does not guarantee safety: records may be incomplete, or investigators may not yet have located the relevant source.

This is the principle described in M01-07: first limit potential harm, then investigate, recover, and improve.

## 6. Connect this lesson to the broader CEH knowledge map

The priority is not memorizing more abbreviations. It is understanding how the related concepts work together.

Figure 7 | Place M09-03 in the wider CEH knowledge structure instead of memorizing three isolated terms.

### Explore the two most important connections

First: MITRE ATT&CK in M01-05.

M01-05 explains that MITRE ATT&CK is not merely a way to divide attacks into stages. It describes attacks through tactics—the objectives—and techniques—the behaviors used.

Using voice communication to persuade a target to provide access can correspond to ATT&CK's [T1566.004 — Spearphishing Voice](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1566/004/). This technique belongs to the Initial Access tactic.

A fake-support call alone, however, does not prove that the attacker successfully obtained initial access. An ATT&CK technique classification describes behavior; the outcome of an incident still requires evidence.

Second: defense in depth in M01-06.

To prevent this type of incident, Northstar cannot rely only on telling employees, “Do not trust calls from strangers.”

It should arrange controls at multiple layers:

| Defensive layer | Example measures |
| --- | --- |
| People and process | Established verification channels, suspicious-request reporting, review of sensitive actions |
| Accounts and authentication | Appropriate phishing-resistant MFA, monitoring for unusual logins |
| Permission management | Least privilege and separate approval for major changes |
| Device protection | Formal authorization for remote-support tools |
| Detection and response | Logs, unusual-activity alerts, session revocation, and incident investigation |

That is the value of defense in depth: even if Lin makes a poor decision in the moment, other controls can reduce the likelihood of attack success and wider harm.

Viewed through M01-01's CIA triad, unauthorized reading of course materials may harm confidentiality; improper alteration of course grades may harm integrity; and an account lockout that interrupts work may harm availability.

## 7. A two-minute exercise: identify a trustworthy source

You do not need to install Kali Linux, run commands, or send real text messages.

Suppose a caller claims to be bank support and asks for a login code received on your phone. How should you verify the request?

A. Call the support number provided in the caller's text

B. Independently open the official banking app you previously verified and use its established entry point

## 8. After-lesson understanding check

These three questions cover attack classification, authentication mechanisms, and incident response. Think through each in your own words; a short answer is enough.

Question 1 | Attack classification

An attacker targets a company's finance manager, first sending a phishing text and then calling while impersonating bank support. Which phishing concepts might apply at the same time, and why?

Question 2 | Technical mechanism

Why can an OTP generated by the genuine platform still help an attacker log in? Why does this not mean that MFA is completely useless?

Question 3 | Incident handling

An employee definitely read out an OTP, but only call records are currently available; login and access logs have not yet been obtained. What can you establish? What can you not establish? What should be done first?

Answer the three questions in your own words, then compare each with its reference explanation. The explanations are teaching material, not records of a learner's submitted answers or verified understanding.

Finally, bring M09-03 together into one complete reasoning approach:

Identify the channel through which the person approached you, the identity they claim, and the access or authority they seek. Then independently verify outside the suspicious source's control. If an unsafe action has already occurred, control the risk first and use evidence to establish the actual impact.

This approach applies not only to fake-support calls, but also to impersonated police or prosecutors, fake managers, account-recovery scams, and future AI voice-impersonation attempts.
